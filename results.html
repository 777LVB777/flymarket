Section 2

The results

These are the final results. Each figure is explained in plain terms below it. All three tests are complete. The learning test passed (section 2.1). The simulated-market test did not pass (section 2.4), and the real-market test did not pass (section 2.5).

2.1

The circuit learns from reward

The circuit was shown two cues, A and B, and rewarded for one of them over 40 training trials. It was then tested five separate times with different random seeds. The pass threshold was set before the run at three times the normal trial-to-trial noise, which came to 6.5 spikes per second.

Figure 1.Change in preference for cue A over cue B after training

Each bar shows how much the circuit's preference for cue A over cue B changed after training, in spikes per second. The thin shaded band near zero is the preset noise threshold. When cue A was rewarded, preference for A rose by 154.5, about 24 times the threshold. When learning was switched off, the change was exactly zero. When cue B was rewarded instead, the preference reversed. When cue A was punished, preference for A fell. Rewarding both cues was included for description only and was not part of the pass rule.

2.2

The circuit tracks price, and learning can trigger runaway firing

Figure 2.Circuit score at different market prices

This figure shows the circuit's score for five different market prices. The score drops steadily as the price rises, so the circuit's response carries the price information. None of the 60 runs fired out of control. A control that sent the same input through different cells produced 68 to 83 percent of the same change, so most of this pattern comes from the strength of the input rather than from which particular cells receive it.

Figure 3.Runaway firing events by training condition

This figure counts the test trials, out of 50 per condition, in which about 3,300 extra neurons suddenly fired together. These events never happened before training, with learning off, or after punishment. They appeared only after reward. When these trials are removed, the main learning result becomes slightly larger (154.5 rises to 159.3), so the result does not depend on them.

This table shows how often the mushroom body "ignited," meaning about 65 percent of the cells that were not being stimulated began firing too. When input cells came from both sides of the brain, this happened in every run with 200 or more cells. With input cells on the left side only, it happened once in 24 runs, even at very high input. For this reason, all later experiments use left-side input only. The both-sides setup was originally caused by a bug, which we found and fixed.

2.4

The simulated-market test did not pass

The circuit was trained on 70 simulated markets and then tested on 180 more, in 20 separate runs, at four strengths of an extra signal: 0 (no information, a control), 0.4, 0.8 and 1.0 (the signal equals the true probability). At each strength it had to beat both the best possible forecast from price alone and its own untrained version, with the 95 percent lower bounds of both improvements above zero. The verdict was published on October 27, 2026.

Table 3.Improvement in forecast error (Brier score) from training, on 3,600 test markets per strength

Signal strengthVersus price alone (95% CI)Versus learning off (95% CI)Result

Positive numbers would mean training helped; every number here is negative. The trained circuit's forecasts were worse than the best price-only forecast and worse than its own untrained version at every signal strength, so the test did not pass. Training also made runaway firing about 30 times more common: about 4.6 percent of trials with learning, against 0.16 percent with learning off. A post-hoc comparison suggests the positive control was difficult to pass with 70 training markets; this was not a preregistered test. The full results, with every reported figure, are in the write-up.

2.5

The real-market test did not pass

The circuit was trained on 70 real Polymarket markets that had already closed, and then tested on 180 later markets it had never seen, in 40 separate runs: – test markets in all, with decisions between March 8 and August 31, 2026. For each market it saw three numbers: the price, how much the price had changed over the previous day, and the number of days until the market was scheduled to close. To pass, its forecasts had to beat both the frozen training-calibrated price and its own learning-off version, with the 95 percent lower bounds of both improvements above zero. The verdict was published on November 8, 2026.

Positive numbers would mean training helped. Both numbers are negative and both intervals lie entirely below zero: the trained circuit's forecasts were worse than the market price and worse than its own untrained version, so the test did not pass. The smallest detectable improvement is the improvement the test had an 80 percent chance of finding at its actual size. The preregistered verdict reads: – Both intervals lie entirely below zero, so for this circuit, set up this way, the data rule out an improvement of any size at the 95 percent level. The verdict's closing clause, written before the result, means only that setups this study did not test (other inputs, more training, a different readout) remain untested. A check fixed in advance found that calibrating the price on the training markets did not make it more accurate there, so the frozen benchmark is the market price itself. The circuit saw only three numbers per market, so this result says nothing about a circuit given outside information. The full results are in the write-up.
